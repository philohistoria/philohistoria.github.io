Week 10 · Nov 19, 2026 · Thursday, 9:00–11:30 am · 280 Brook Street, Room 110

AI governance

Authors: Han Zhang and Codex (OpenAI)

Compare policy alternatives and defend a recommendation while explaining how AI contributed to the analysis.

Opening

Today

  • Use Bradford’s comparative framework to introduce the governance discussion.
  • Groups investigate one of six areas: frontier-model risk; jurisdiction and regimes; geopolitics and chips; power and concentration; the information ecosystem; or labor and the economy.
  • Use agentic AI tools to locate and organize sources. Check the claims used in the presentation.

Readings

Readings

  • Bradford, Digital Empires (2023), Introduction.
  • One core reading selected by your group for its debate. Bring the citation and source. The AI Index policy chapter and the existing reading pool are references.

Comparative governance

The comparative framework

Use Bradford’s market-driven, state-driven, and rights-driven models to compare the policy approaches in the cases. Check the dates and sources of the rules your group discusses.

The six debates

Frontier-model risk

  • Core reading options:
    • International AI Safety Report.
    • "Backfiring Effect of Weak AI Safety Regulation," PNAS (2026).

Jurisdiction and regimes

  • Core reading options:
    • Nelson, Science (2026).
    • Matt Sheehan, "China's AI Regulations and How They Get Made," Carnegie (2023), on how the rules are made.
    • Migliorini, "China's Interim Measures on Generative AI" (2024), on what the rules say and how they compare with the EU AI Act.
  • Case material: the December 2025 executive order on preemption.

Geopolitics and chips

  • Core reading: an export-control brief, chosen by the group with the instructor.

Power and concentration

  • Core reading option: Kak and West.
  • Choose one core reading that directly addresses the group’s policy issue.

Information ecosystem

  • Core reading: Kostka and Bogs, "The Expanding Digital Reach of the Chinese State" (2026).
  • The mechanism is function creep: a system built for one purpose is used for another.
    • Health codes repurposed against protesters.
    • Facial recognition used on Uyghurs and on schoolchildren.
  • Rules to compare:
    • China's 2025 labeling rules.
    • Article 50 of the EU AI Act.
    • State deepfake-election laws in the United States.

Labor and economy

  • Core reading options:
    • Brynjolfsson, Mitchell and Rock (2018), available in the reading reference pool.
    • Bradford, "False Choice" (2024).
    • A Draghi excerpt.
    • Jia, Jin and Wagman (2021).

Group presentations · do it on the right

What each group does

  • Each group speaks for 8 to 10 minutes.
  • Four things in every presentation:
    • explain the dispute,
    • say who is on each side,
    • give one concrete case with its source and date,
    • share one core reading.
  • The core reading comes from the menu above, or your group finds one with the agent.
  • In class, build a comparison table for at least two policy alternatives. State the criteria and the evidence behind each entry.
  • Every presentation ends with a recommendation for a named decision-maker, and the trade-off most relevant to that choice.

Steps 6 and 7: weigh the approaches, and decide

The policy track today

  • Step 6 of the eightfold path is confronting the trade-offs among the alternatives.
  • Step 7 is deciding.
  • Today the alternatives are the approaches to regulating AI, one debate per group.
  • The decision you write down addresses your group’s governance issue and a named decision-maker.

Project and policy discussion

Alternatives, trade-offs, and decisions

  • What does each alternative improve, and what costs or risks does it introduce?
  • Would a different criterion change the recommendation?
  • How did AI contribute to the group’s evidence, alternatives, or comparison?

Assignments

Weekly memo · due Dec 3 before class. Write 1–2 observations from your project or the classroom case: the policy-analysis step you attempted, how AI helped or fell short, and what you would do next. Aim for 150–200 words total. Describe what you actually tried and observed in your own words. Do not use AI to write the memo. Begin in class and submit to Canvas Discussions. See memo grading.

Hands-on

Step 1

Present your debate

Eight to ten minutes. Cover the four points in order: the dispute, the sides, one sourced case, and your core reading.

Step 2

Build the comparison table

  • One row for each of at least two policy alternatives.
  • One column for each criterion you are using.
  • In each cell, the evidence behind the entry and its source.
Step 3

Close with the shared question

End on the question every group ends on:

  • Which policy alternative do you recommend, for which decision-maker, and why?
  • Which trade-off matters most for that choice?
Step 4

Take notes on the other five

  • Write down each group's answer to the closing question.
  • Use the discussion to revise the alternatives, criteria, and recommendation in your group’s comparison table.
Keep your group’s comparison table and the sources used in the discussion.
