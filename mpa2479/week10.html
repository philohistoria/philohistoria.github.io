Authors: Han Zhang and Codex (OpenAI)

Opening

Today

  • Today asks how governments should make rules for AI. This topic is called AI governance.
  • Congress and many states are writing new laws on AI. A project at Brown tracks these bills.
  • You learn to weigh policy alternatives against each other and to recommend one.
  • Three pairs each present one debate about how to govern AI.
  • The class puts the three presentations side by side in one table.

AI bills in the United States

Example: a Maryland law on election deepfakes

  • A deepfake is a fake image, video, or audio recording made with AI. It shows a real person saying or doing something they never said or did.
  • In 2026 Maryland passed a law on deepfakes in elections, Senate Bill 141 (MD SB141). It does two things:
    • It prohibits creating or spreading a deepfake to produce materially false information, knowingly or with reckless disregard.
    • It requires the State Administrator of Elections to publish correct information after a credible report of election misinformation.
  • Many states and Congress are writing laws like this one.

The CNTR AISLE Bill Library

  • Brown’s Center for Technological Responsibility, Reimagination and Redesign (CNTR) tracks these bills. Its Bill Library listed 6,921 federal and state bills in November 2026.
  • You can filter the bills by state, status, and date. Each bill has a page with a summary and a link to the full text.
  • Analysts describe each bill with yes-or-no questions of the form “Does the bill …?”. The questions fall into seven areas:
    • accountability and transparency,
    • data protection,
    • bias and discrimination,
    • labor force,
    • institutions,
    • education,
    • synthetic content, such as deepfakes.
  • Each bill page shows the share of questions answered “yes” in each area. For the Maryland law, the share is 32% for synthetic content and 8% for accountability and transparency. It is 0% in the other areas.
  • The questions work like your Week 5 codebook. Each answer is a code assigned by an analyst.
  • A share of zero does not always mean the bill has no rules in that area. The “How It Works” page lists the reasons.
  • Use the Bill Library to find a concrete case for your presentation. Open the full text of the bill and check the summary against it.

Trade-offs and policy decisions

Trade-offs between policy alternatives

  • States have taken two approaches to AI in election campaigns:
    • Maryland bans deceptive deepfakes (MD SB141, above).
    • Louisiana requires campaigns to disclose the use of AI in telephone campaign messages (LA HB639, 2026).
  • Suppose a state legislature is choosing between a ban and a disclosure requirement.
  • Suppose the evidence supports the entries in this table.
AlternativeProtects voters from deceptionLeaves room for political speech
Ban on deceptive deepfakesMoreLess
Disclosure requirementLessMore
  • The ban and the disclosure requirement are courses of action the legislature could take. As in Week 9, each one is a policy alternative.
  • Protection from deception and room for political speech are the standards used to judge them. These are the criteria.
  • The ban gives up some political speech to gain protection from deception. The disclosure requirement does the reverse.
  • Neither alternative is best on both criteria. To gain on one criterion, the legislature gives up something on another. This is called a trade-off.
  • State a trade-off in that form: what each alternative gains, and what it gives up.
  • Sometimes one alternative is at least as good as another on every criterion. Then those two have no trade-off. Drop the weaker one.
  • Confronting the trade-offs is Step 6 of the eightfold path.

Policy decisions and recommendations

  • After weighing the trade-offs, you choose one alternative. You also say who should adopt it.
  • In the example, the state legislature has the power to adopt the ban or the disclosure requirement. The person or body with that power is the decision-maker.
  • Other examples of decision-makers:
    • a governor,
    • a federal agency,
    • the European Commission.
  • A recommendation has three parts:
    • the decision-maker,
    • the alternative you recommend,
    • the main reason, with its evidence.
  • Name the trade-off that matters most for that choice. A decision-maker who weighs that trade-off differently would choose differently.
  • The best choice depends on the decision-maker. The European Commission and a U.S. governor can weigh the same criteria differently.
  • If you cannot choose, go back to an earlier step. Look for one of these:
    • more evidence,
    • a new alternative,
    • a clearer criterion.
  • Deciding is Step 7 of the eightfold path.
  • Bardach and Patashnik describe both steps in Part I of A Practical Guide for Policy Analysis.

Presentation format · do it on the right

Contents of a presentation

  • Each pair presents one debate in AI governance.
  • Students present in three pairs. Each pair speaks for 12 to 15 minutes.
  • Each presentation covers six points:
    • what the dispute is about,
    • who is on each side,
    • one concrete case, with its source and date,
    • the core reading,
    • a comparison of at least two policy alternatives, with the criteria and the evidence behind each,
    • a recommendation for a named decision-maker, with the trade-off that matters most for that choice.
  • Use the agent to find and organize sources.
  • Check every claim in the presentation against its source.
