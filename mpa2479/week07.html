Fairness metrics

Three definitions, and what each one holds equal

  • One concern about algorithmic bias is that a system's errors fall unevenly across groups. Specify the outcome, the groups, and the error rate you are comparing.
  • Use these criteria to distinguish selection rates, false positive rates, and outcomes among those flagged. The readings emphasize different comparisons.
DefinitionPlain EnglishExample
Demographic parity Each group gets flagged at the same rate. 30% of Group A and 30% of Group B are predicted "high risk."
Equalized false positive rates Among people without the observed outcome, each group has the same chance of being wrongly flagged. Among people without the recorded outcome, 10% of each group are flagged.
Predictive parity Among flagged cases, each group has the same recorded outcome rate. Among flagged cases, 60% of each group have the recorded outcome.

Equalized odds requires equal false positive and true positive rates across groups. Score calibration asks whether people given the same score have the same observed outcome rate; this differs from predictive parity for everyone above a threshold.

Compare what each criterion reveals

  • The base rate is the share of a group for whom the observed outcome occurs.
  • Compute each measure for the same example. A similar rate on one measure can coexist with a substantial difference on another.
  • Discuss who bears the costs of false positives and false negatives, and which comparison matters for the proposed policy use.
  • Name the criterion you use, report the other relevant rates, and explain whether the outcome labels themselves are suitable for this decision.

Subgroup error audit · do it on the right

The COMPAS scores, recomputed by group

  • ProPublica published the risk scores it analyzed and the code it used, in its COMPAS analysis GitHub repository (CSV).
  • Apply the sample filters in ProPublica’s analysis notebook and record which rows they drop.
  • In Week 5 you compared coded labels against a reference in a confusion matrix. Here, combine the Medium and High categories as a positive prediction and treat Low as negative, following the notebook. Use two_year_recid as the recorded outcome.
  • A matrix computed over the whole sample reports the average error and hides where the error falls. Recompute it separately for each group.
  • Compare the false positive rate and predictive precision across groups. In this exercise, a flagged case has a Medium or High score.
  • A subgroup rate is only as good as the number of cases in that subgroup, so report the counts alongside the rates.

Discussion

  • Compare two policy alternatives: using the score to inform a specified decision, or making that decision without it. Describe how each option would work.
  • Which errors matter for each option, and who bears their costs?
  • Which fairness criterion would you use to compare the options? What evidence would you need about the alternative process?
  • What does the recorded two-year outcome capture, and what would you need to know about its measurement?

Project and policy discussion

Errors, criteria, and trade-offs

  • How would an error change your finding or recommendation?
  • What criterion is each side of the fairness debate using? Which matters for the decision being made?
  • Which changes would strengthen your own analysis, and how much has AI helped with checking it?
