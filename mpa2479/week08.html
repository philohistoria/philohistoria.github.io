The White House AI memo

The White House AI memo

  • In April 2025, the White House sent federal agencies a memo on their use of AI.
  • The memo came from the Office of Management and Budget (OMB). OMB is the White House office that oversees the budgets and management of federal agencies.
  • Its title is Accelerating Federal Use of AI through Innovation, Governance, and Public Trust.
  • The memo tells agencies how to adopt AI and which safeguards some AI systems need.
  • It also requires each agency to publish a list of its AI systems every year (p. 12).
  • This page calls it the White House AI memo. Its official number is M-25-21.

Definition of AI in the White House memo

  • AI includes (p. 18):
    • machine learning, including deep learning, supervised, unsupervised, and reinforcement learning,
    • generative AI,
    • statistical models that predict from data, such as a risk score.
  • Simple models count too. The memo says a model’s size, type, or amount of training data does not exclude it.
  • A system counts whether or not a person reviews its output.
  • AI does not include:
    • systems that follow only rules written by people, such as a scoring formula with weights set by hand,
    • software that repeats a person’s steps exactly, called robotic process automation,
    • common commercial products with AI built in, such as a word processor or a map app.
  • The Department of Defense and the intelligence agencies do not list individual systems (pp. 12 and 25).
  • Each agency decides which of its systems to list. The list shows what agencies reported. It cannot show what they left out.

Public disclosure of federal AI

  • OMB combines the agency lists into one public list on GitHub.
  • The list is in the Data folder of that repository, with one row per system. Use the file 2025_individually_reported_AI_use_cases.
    • The .xlsx version opens in Excel. Use it to look through the rows.
    • The .csv version is for the agent.
  • As of April 13, 2026, the list has 3,611 systems.
  • Agencies marked 445 of them high-impact. A later section explains this label.
  • OMB calls each entry a use case and the list an inventory.
  • The data dictionary describes each column of the inventory.

Exercise 1: Description and classification of federal AI systems · do it on the right

Counts by agency, topic area, and stage

  • Which agencies use AI, and for what? Start with the whole list.
  • Use your agent to count the systems three ways, with a table and a bar chart for each:
    • by agency,
    • by topic area, the list’s own grouping of what each system is used for,
    • by stage of development, such as pilot or deployed.
  • Many rows leave the topic area or the stage of development blank. Decide how to report those rows.

Classification of what the systems do

  • The topic area says little about what a system does to people.
  • Each row also has a short text, written by the agency, on the problem the system solves.
  • Use that text to classify the systems yourself, with the text-coding method from Week 5. Possible categories:
    • decisions or scores about individual people, such as benefits, enforcement, or immigration,
    • internal agency work, such as translation or document processing,
    • science and research,
    • other.
  • Write your own codebook. You may change these categories.
  • Everyone codes the same 31 systems: the first deployed system listed for each agency. This way you can compare results.
  • Then compare your codes with a classmate’s.
  • Recall Week 5. The codebook, the wording of the prompt, and the choice of model all changed the codes you got. The same holds here.
  • For each system you coded differently, read its text. Find the choice behind the difference.

Risks of government AI

Example: disability benefit reviews

  • The Social Security Administration pays disability benefits.
  • It uses a computer model to pick which people to review.
  • The model looks for people whose health may have improved.
  • A review can end a person’s benefits.
  • If the model is wrong, people can lose benefits they need.
  • The agency reports this model, the “Continuing Disability Review Model”, in the public list of federal AI systems.

Example: child-welfare screening in Allegheny County

  • Local governments use such models too.
  • Allegheny County, Pennsylvania, runs a hotline for reports of child abuse and neglect.
  • Since 2016, a model has scored each call. Staff use the score to decide which families to investigate.
  • Virginia Eubanks studied this model in Automating Inequality (2018), chapter 4.
  • She argues that the model’s data come mostly from public services. Families who use those services, who are mostly poor, therefore look riskier to the model.
  • This is a county system. It does not appear in the federal list.

Consequences of errors

  • COMPAS, from Week 7, is a risk score used in criminal justice. Week 7 showed that its errors differed across groups.
  • Federal systems such as the disability review model also shape decisions about people’s benefits, rights, or safety.
  • When such a system is wrong, the cost falls on the people the decision is about.
  • For this reason, the White House AI memo sets extra rules for these systems. The next section describes them.

Federal rules for high-impact AI

Definition of high-impact AI

  • The memo sets extra rules for AI that affects people’s rights or safety.
  • The memo calls such AI high-impact.
  • In the memo’s words, AI is high-impact “when its output serves as a principal basis for decisions or actions that have a legal, material, binding, or significant effect on rights or safety” (p. 14).
  • Section 6 lists uses that the memo presumes to be high-impact (pp. 21–22).
  • One of them is a risk assessment about individuals in law enforcement. COMPAS, from Week 7, is that kind of tool.
  • Each agency decides for itself whether its system is high-impact.

Required risk-management practices

  • High-impact AI must come with safeguards. Section 4(b) of the memo lists seven of them (pp. 15–17):
    • testing before the system is used,
    • an assessment of the system’s effects on people, called an AI impact assessment,
    • ongoing monitoring of how well the system works and of any harm it causes,
    • training and assessment for the staff who operate the system,
    • human oversight, intervention, and accountability,
    • remedies or appeals for the people affected,
    • feedback from end users and the public.
  • The memo calls these seven safeguards the minimum risk-management practices.
  • For each deployed high-impact system, the inventory reports what the agency says it did for each practice.

Exercise 2: Audit of one high-impact system · do it on the right

What an audit is

  • The White House AI memo requires seven practices for every high-impact system.
  • The public list reports, for each deployed high-impact system, what the agency says it did for each practice.
  • An audit checks one system against these requirements. For each practice, it asks two questions:
    • What does the agency report?
    • Is there a document that lets the public verify it?

Example: an audit of the disability review model

  • This example audits the Social Security Administration’s disability review model, described above under Risks of government AI.
  • The public list has one row for this model. To find it, open the .xlsx file in the Data folder and search for “Continuing Disability Review Model”.
  • The middle column below shows what SSA wrote in that row. The last column shows the instructor’s judgment, based on that row alone.

  • SSA reports that it meets every practice.
  • The row links no document. The privacy impact assessment and code fields are empty.
  • The impact assessment describes what the model does for SSA. It says nothing about risks to the people whose benefits are reviewed.
  • The conclusion: the public can see only the agency’s own answers. Nothing in the public record lets anyone verify them.

Your audit

  • Audit a different system in the same way.
  • Choose a deployed system that its agency marked high-impact.
  • Many such systems leave the practice columns blank. Record that as a finding.
  • If your system’s row links to documents, open them. They may let you judge a practice as met, or show a gap.
  • Make the same table: one row per practice, with what the agency reports, any document, and your own judgment.
  • Your judgment is one of three: the requirement is met, there is a gap, or there is not enough information to judge.
  • Where information is missing, say what document would settle the question.

Comparing audit findings

Findings across systems

  • Each student speaks for two minutes about their system. Cover these points:
    • what the system does and which agency runs it,
    • why it meets the high-impact definition,
    • the practice with the strongest evidence behind it,
    • the practice where the public record says least,
    • the document or inventory column you read for each of those two practices.
  • Then compare the systems. Discuss these questions:
    • Does a documented gap recur across systems?
    • Where is public information insufficient?
    • Did a reported practice ever come with a document you could open?
    • What further evidence would show that an agency carries out a practice it reports?

Limits of the public record

  • Many rows may end with “not enough information to judge”.
  • That result is itself a finding. It describes what the public can learn about government AI.
  • It raises policy questions:
    • Should agencies publish more about their high-impact systems?
    • Should someone outside the agency audit these systems? Who?
    • What would each option cost, and what would it reveal?

Hands-on

Step 1

Download the list

  • Download the CSV file of the list from the Data folder into your repository.
  • Keep the raw file unchanged.
  • Record where and when you downloaded it.
Step 2

Count the systems

  • Use your agent to count the systems by agency, by topic area, and by stage of development.
  • Make a table and a bar chart for each.
  • The data dictionary explains each column.
  • Find out how your agent treated blank cells.
  • Check one count by hand in the .xlsx file.
Step 3

Write a codebook

  • Read the problem_solved text of a few systems.
  • Write a codebook: each category, its definition, and one example.
Step 4

Code 31 systems

  • For each agency, take the first system in the file whose stage of development is “Deployed”. This gives 31 systems. Everyone uses the same 31.
  • Code their problem_solved text with a model, as in Week 5.
  • Hand-code ten of them yourself. Compare your codes with the model’s.
Step 5

Compare with a classmate

  • Compare your codes for the 31 systems with a classmate’s.
  • For each system you coded differently, read its text.
  • Decide whether the difference comes from the codebook, the prompt, or the model.
  • Write what you found in your README.
Step 1

Find the high-impact systems

  • Use your agent to list the systems that their agency marked high-impact.
  • Include each system’s agency, stage of development, and stated purpose.
  • Choose one deployed system to audit, other than the SSA model in the example.
  • Many deployed high-impact systems leave the practice columns blank. An audit of such a system is useful too.
Step 2

Read your system’s entry

  • Read every column of your system’s row. Use the data dictionary for the meaning of each column.
  • Find the columns that report the required practices. Their names begin with hi_. Seven of them match the seven practices. Two more give detail on the impact assessment.
  • An answer can say that a practice is in progress, or that the agency’s Chief AI Officer waived it.
  • Note any links to agency documents.
Optional

Follow a linked document

If your entry links to an agency document, open it for more evidence. If a page will not open, record that as a limitation.

Step 3

Build the audit table

  • Save the White House AI memo PDF in your repository.
  • With your agent, make a table with one row per required practice.
  • In each row, give the practice, its section in the memo, what the list reports, and a judgment: met, gap, or not enough information.
  • Where nothing is reported, say so. Do not let the agent fill the gap with a guess.
Step 4

Make your own judgments

  • Read each row against the evidence it cites.
  • Keep, change, or reject each judgment the agent proposed.
  • Where nothing is reported, write one sentence on what the public cannot learn about this system.
Step 5

Check the agent’s reading of the memo

  • Check the table in a new conversation or with a subagent, as in Week 3.
  • Each row should match a sentence in the memo.
  • Find each sentence in the PDF yourself.
